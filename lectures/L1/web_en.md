## @intro

A bank decides every day whom to lend to, a card issuer which payment to block, a trader how much risk to take. Deep learning, the branch of artificial intelligence (AI) that fits long chains of simple functions to data, now sits behind many of these decisions. This first lecture starts from the models every analyst already knows, linear and logistic regression, and shows that they are neural networks with a single neuron. From there we build Rosenblatt's perceptron, see where it fails, and arrive at the multilayer perceptron (MLP), the network behind every later lecture.

Every number on this page is computed on real, public credit data: 1,000 applicants of the German credit data, 5,960 home equity loans (HMEQ) and, for one picture of function approximation, 20,640 Californian census block groups. The case study compares logistic regression, an MLP and gradient boosting with the same protocol, several random seeds, intervals and the cost of errors, because a model choice in a bank is a decision under uncertainty, not a contest of single numbers.

# Deep learning for business

Why deep learning matters now, where it creates value in finance and business, where a simpler model is enough, and the data we use.

## Deep learning learns the features that analysts used to build by hand {h=44779ba0}

A deep network is a composition of layers, $f(\mathbf x;\boldsymbol\theta)=f^{(L)}(f^{(L-1)}(\cdots f^{(1)}(\mathbf x)))$, whose parameters $\boldsymbol\theta$ are all chosen to minimise the average loss on the training data. What changed in the last fifteen years is not the idea but its scale: large labelled datasets, graphics processing units (GPU) and software that differentiates automatically, such as PyTorch. The network no longer needs features designed by an analyst; it learns its own representations from raw inputs (LeCun, Bengio and Hinton, 2015).

@practice ask what the network would learn that a scorecard analyst cannot write down by hand.

## Where deep learning creates value in business and finance {h=a6d75137}

The course is organised by decision and data type. Credit and payments are tabular problems (Lectures 1 to 3); market and energy forecasting are sequences (Lectures 5 and 13); news, filings and central-bank speeches are text (Lectures 6, 7 and 12); payment networks are graphs (Lecture 9). The rule is always the same: start from the decision and its cost, then choose the network that matches the data.

## For small tabular data, a linear model or boosting is often enough {h=8831f558}

Deep learning is not automatically better. In the large credit-scoring benchmark of Lessmann et al. (2015), logistic regression stays a solid reference and ensembles lead; on typical medium-sized tabular data, tree ensembles still beat deep networks (Grinsztajn, Oyallon and Varoquaux, 2022). Credit decisions must also be explained to the applicant. This lecture therefore reports every network next to a linear and a boosting benchmark.

## Three real credit datasets, used without any simulated record {h=6855fc6c}

German credit comes from the University of California, Irvine (UCI) Machine Learning Repository: 1,000 applicants, 20 attributes, amounts in Deutsche Mark (DM) and a default rate of 30.0 %. HMEQ (Baesens, Rösch and Scheule, 2016) has 5,960 loans, a default rate of 19.9 % and many missing values, which we keep. California housing gives median house values in units of 100,000 US dollars (USD).

@practice check the source, the unit of observation and the event rate before any model.

## Default rates differ strongly across simple applicant groups {h=43f1d73c}

The simplest risk model is the default rate per group, $\widehat{\mathrm{PD}}_g=\frac1{n_g}\sum_{i\in g}y_i$, where the probability of default (PD) of group $g$ is the share of its loans with $y_i=1$. In German credit, applicants with a negative checking balance default in 49.3 % of cases, those without a checking account in 11.7 %. In HMEQ, the rate climbs from 14.0 % with no delinquent credit line to 67.3 % with three or more.

@chart default_rates
Default rate by checking-account status (German credit) and by number of delinquent credit lines (HMEQ); dashed: sample average.

@practice a model must at least reproduce these group rates on the validation set.

## Learning = minimising the average loss on training data, judged on new data {h=7df41c19}

Training minimises the empirical risk $\hat R_n(\boldsymbol\theta)=\frac1n\sum_i\ell(y_i,f(\mathbf x_i;\boldsymbol\theta))$, but the bank pays the risk on future applicants, $R(\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathbb E\,\ell(y,f(\mathbf x;\boldsymbol\theta))$. We therefore split the data in three: training ($\boldsymbol\theta$), validation (model choice and stopping) and test (reported once). For HMEQ the stratified split gives 3,576, 1,192 and 1,192 loans; medians and scaling are fitted on the training part only, so no information leaks from the test set.

## @end

Deep learning creates most value on raw, high-dimensional inputs; on small engineered tables it must beat logistic regression and boosting under a fixed protocol, on data that stay real from the first table to the last test.

# Linear models are one-neuron networks

Linear and logistic regression are a single neuron with an identity or a sigmoid output; their losses are likelihoods and their gradients have the form that backpropagation will repeat in every layer.

## Linear regression is one neuron with no activation {h=9c17b689}

A neuron without activation computes $\hat y=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x+b$. With Gaussian errors, maximising the likelihood is the same as minimising the mean squared error (MSE), $\frac1n\sum(y_i-\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_i-b)^2$, which has the closed form $\hat{\mathbf w}=(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf y$. Networks lose this closed form and are trained by gradient descent (Lecture 2).

## Logistic regression is one neuron with a sigmoid {h=f47347cf}

With a sigmoid output, $p_i=\sigma(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_i+b)$, and the Bernoulli negative log-likelihood (cross-entropy) as loss, the neuron is logistic regression:

$$ \mathcal L(\mathbf w,b)=-\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big[y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\big],\qquad \nabla_{\mathbf w}\mathcal L=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(p_i-y_i)\,\mathbf x_i $$ {#eq:logit}

The gradient @eq:logit is the error times the input; the loss is convex, so there is a single minimum, and $e^{w_j}$ is the odds ratio of input $j$.

## Delinquencies and a missing debt ratio raise the odds of default most {h=cb072f75}

With standardised inputs, the coefficients are changes in log-odds per standard deviation and can be compared. The odds ratios are 2.89 for a missing debt-to-income ratio, 2.17 for more delinquent lines and 0.65 for an older credit history; McFadden's pseudo-$R^2$ is 0.374.

@chart logit_coef
Logistic regression on HMEQ: standardised coefficients with 95 % confidence intervals.

@practice a sign against credit sense is a reason to inspect the data, not to drop the model.

## The forward pass of one real applicant, step by step {h=50dfa953}

For one test applicant who later defaulted (a loan of 15,400 USD, debt-to-income 36.4 %, 2 delinquent lines), the score is the sum of the contributions $w_jx_j$ plus the bias, $z=$ −1.71, and the neuron outputs $p=\sigma(z)=$ 15.3 %. The applicant would pass a 50 % cut-off; Section 5 shows why the cut-off should be set from costs.

## A missing value can be the strongest signal {h=875df299}

The debt-to-income ratio is missing for 21.3 % of HMEQ loans, and these default in 62.0 % of cases against 8.6 % when it is reported. Dropping incomplete rows would drop the riskiest applicants. We fill each numeric input with its training median and add an indicator of missingness, which gives $d=$ 28 inputs.

@practice never drop rows with missing values in credit data.

## @end

One neuron with the right output and loss is a classical statistical model; the network view adds nothing yet, but it fixes the notation and the gradient that every deeper model reuses.

# The perceptron

Rosenblatt's perceptron learns a linear boundary from its mistakes. It converges when the classes are separable and fails otherwise, which is the usual case in credit, and it cannot represent XOR.

## From a logical neuron to a learning machine {h=31f948ac}

McCulloch and Pitts (1943) wrote the neuron as a threshold unit, $\hat y=\operatorname{step}(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x+b)$; Rosenblatt (1958) made its weights learn from labelled examples. Minsky and Papert (1969) proved that one layer cannot represent XOR, and the field recovered only when backpropagation trained several layers (Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams, 1986).

## The perceptron learns only from its mistakes {h=4b7f4068}

If example $i$ is misclassified, $y_i(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_i+b)\le0$ with $y_i\in\{-1,+1\}$, the weights move towards it: $\mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w+\eta y_i\mathbf x_i$. This is one step of stochastic gradient descent (SGD) on the perceptron criterion. Correct examples give no gradient and the output is a class, not a probability, which a lender cannot price.

## If the classes can be separated, the perceptron stops after finitely many mistakes {h=c78cbff2}

Novikoff (1962): if a unit vector $\mathbf w^*$ separates the data with margin $\gamma$ and all inputs lie within radius $R$, the perceptron makes at most $(R/\gamma)^2$ mistakes, whatever the number of examples or the dimension. Without a separating hyperplane the theorem says nothing.

## On real loans the perceptron never settles {h=5c326982}

On the HMEQ training loans the classes overlap. Over 60 epochs the training error jumps between 13.2 % and 23.4 % after 35,876 updates, while logistic regression on the same inputs reaches 11.7 % and always predicting 'no default' 19.9 %.

@chart perceptron_errors
Training error of the perceptron after each epoch on HMEQ, against logistic regression and the majority rule.

@practice a smooth loss and a probability output fix both problems.

## No straight line separates XOR: one hidden layer does {h=c2363946}

XOR is true when exactly one input is on. Any linear boundary classifies at most three of the four points correctly (50 % here), while an MLP with two tanh hidden units reaches 100 %: the hidden layer maps the inputs to a space where the classes become separable. Interactions such as 'high debt and short history' are of this kind. This is a mechanism picture on the logical table, not a data result.

@chart xor
XOR: one linear boundary (left) and an MLP with two hidden units (right).

## @end

The perceptron is a linear classifier that learns only from mistakes; it converges on separable data and cycles on real, overlapping classes. Smooth losses, probability outputs and hidden layers remove these limits.

# The multilayer perceptron

Stacking affine maps and nonlinear activations gives the MLP: we write its forward pass, choose activations and outputs, count parameters, and see what the universal approximation theorem does and does not promise.

## An MLP alternates affine maps and nonlinear activations {h=c7ae780e}

Each hidden layer computes $\mathbf h^{(l)}=a(\mathbf W^{(l)}\mathbf h^{(l-1)}+\mathbf b^{(l)})$, starting from $\mathbf h^{(0)}=\mathbf x$, and the output layer is a logistic regression on the last hidden features. Without the activation $a$ the layers collapse into one linear map. Our credit network has $d=$ 28 inputs and two hidden layers of 64 rectified linear units (ReLU).

## Activation functions: smooth, cheap, and with a usable gradient {h=2b9aa09b}

Sigmoid and tanh saturate: the derivative of the sigmoid is at most 0.25 and both derivatives vanish for large inputs, which slows learning in deep networks (Lecture 2). ReLU, $\max(0,z)$, has slope 1 for positive inputs and is the default in hidden layers; the Gaussian error linear unit (GELU), $z\Phi(z)$, is its smooth variant used in transformers.

@chart activations
Activation functions (left) and their derivatives (right); analytic curves.

## The output layer and the loss come from the type of the target {h=73df7fd4}

The output and the loss follow from the distribution of the target: identity and MSE for a real number, sigmoid and cross-entropy for a binary event, softmax and categorical cross-entropy for $K$ classes, and the pinball loss for a quantile, such as the value at risk (VaR) of Lecture 2. Each pair is a maximum-likelihood estimator.

## A small MLP already has hundreds of times more parameters than the logistic score {h=45f47cd2}

The number of parameters is $\sum_l(H_{l-1}+1)H_l$. On HMEQ the logistic model has 29 parameters and the MLP 64-64 has 6,081, for 3,576 training loans: enough to memorise the training defaults, which is why every network keeps the weights of its best validation epoch.

## One hidden layer can approximate any continuous function {h=c4bb4c85}

For any continuous function on a compact set and any tolerance, a one-hidden-layer network with enough units approximates it (Cybenko, 1989; Hornik, 1991). The theorem does not say how many units are needed, whether gradient descent finds the weights, or whether they generalise.

## More hidden units trace the price geography of California {h=b2a6a0cc}

House values in California peak around Los Angeles and San Francisco. Fitting them on latitude alone, the held-out MSE falls from 0.896 for a straight line to 0.855, 0.757 and 0.731 with 1, 3 and 50 sigmoid units. The 992 block groups at the source cap of 500,000 USD are left out.

@chart uat_california
House values against latitude: bin means and fitted networks with 1, 3 and 50 hidden units.

@practice location effects are nonlinear: a linear term cannot price them.

## Depth reuses features: deep and narrow can beat shallow and wide {h=2bebfff6}

A ReLU network is piecewise linear, and the number of its linear regions can grow exponentially with depth for a fixed number of units (Montúfar et al., 2014). On tabular credit data, two or three hidden layers are usually enough; depth pays off on images, text and sequences.

## An MLP bends the decision boundary, but two inputs carry little extra signal {h=766f3ff1}

With two HMEQ inputs, debt-to-income and the age of the oldest credit line, the logistic model draws straight probability bands and the MLP curved regions. The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), the probability that a random defaulter scores above a random good loan, is 0.744 for the logistic model and 0.736 for the MLP: flexibility does not help when the two inputs carry little extra signal.

@chart boundary_hmeq
Predicted PD on two HMEQ variables: logistic regression (left) and MLP 64-64 (right), with test loans.

## @end

An MLP is a stack of affine maps and activations; ReLU in the hidden layers, an output and loss that match the target, and a parameter count that tells how much data and regularisation are needed.

# Case study: credit scoring with real loans

Logistic regression, an MLP and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) are compared on HMEQ and German credit with the same splits, inputs and metrics, several seeds, bootstrap intervals, a corrected test, calibration and the cost of errors.

## The protocol: same split, same inputs, three models {h=c39197d0}

The MLPs use ReLU layers, Adam with learning rate 0.001 and the weights of the best validation epoch, each with 5 seeds; XGBoost uses depth 4, learning rate 0.03 and early stopping on validation. HMEQ has one stratified 60/20/20 split; German credit uses 5 times repeated 5-fold cross-validation (25 test folds). All models trained on the central processing unit (CPU) with seed 20261108.

## On HMEQ, a wider hidden layer raises the test AUC above the logistic score {h=83bb93cb}

With one hidden layer, the test AUC rises from 0.901 with one unit, essentially a logistic model, to 0.953 with 256 units; logistic regression reaches 0.920 and XGBoost 0.961. The gain flattens after about 16 units and the seed spread shrinks as the network widens.

@chart width_auc
Test AUC on HMEQ against the number of hidden units (mean and range of 5 seeds), with the two benchmarks.

## At a 10 % false-alarm rate, the MLP catches more defaults than the logistic score {h=c253d27c}

The ROC curve plots the share of defaults caught against the share of good loans rejected for every cut-off. When 10 % of good loans are rejected, logistic regression catches 83.2 % of the 238 test defaults, the MLP 85.3 % and XGBoost 90.8 %.

@chart roc_hmeq
ROC curves on the HMEQ test set.

@practice read the curve at the rejection rate the business accepts.

## The MLP gain on HMEQ is larger than seed noise and test-set noise {h=da74e6ed}

The MLP 64-64 reaches a mean test AUC of 0.945 over 5 seeds (standard deviation 0.004). A paired bootstrap of the test loans gives the AUC gain over logistic regression, 0.023, a 95 % interval of [0.008; 0.037], which excludes zero.

## The scores are close to calibrated probabilities {h=b571d62a}

Provisions and loan prices use the PD itself, so the probabilities must be right in level, not only in order. In each decile of predicted PD the observed default rate is close to the predicted one; the Brier scores are 0.0742 for logistic regression, 0.0625 for the MLP and 0.0549 for XGBoost, against 0.1598 for a constant rate.

@chart calibration
Reliability diagram on the HMEQ test set: predicted against observed default rate by decile.

## On 1,000 German applicants, the MLP does not beat logistic regression {h=8d28d91e}

On German credit the picture changes: mean AUC 0.777 for logistic regression, 0.766 for the MLP and 0.778 for XGBoost across 25 held-out folds. With so few applicants the network has nothing to add.

@chart german_cv
AUC of each held-out fold in 5 x 5-fold cross-validation on German credit; bars are means.

## Why the usual t-test overstates differences in cross-validation {h=e97242b1}

The fold scores are correlated because the training sets overlap, so the naive t-test is too optimistic. The Nadeau-Bengio correction inflates the variance by $1/J+n_{\text{test}}/n_{\text{train}}$; the MLP against logistic regression gives $t=$ −0.93 and $p=$ 0.362, so there is no evidence of a difference.

## A missed default costs five times a rejected good applicant: cut at 1/6, not 1/2 {h=a710fe48}

The UCI cost matrix weighs a missed bad risk 5 times a rejected good applicant. Rejecting when $5p\ge1-p$ gives the cut-off $1/6$. With out-of-fold predictions, the logistic model costs 0.554 per applicant at 1/6 and 0.870 at 1/2; the MLP 0.568 and 1.130; accepting everyone costs 1.50.

@chart cost_threshold
Cost per German credit applicant against the cut-off, out-of-fold predictions; dashed: cut-off 1/6.

@practice the cut-off is where most money is lost or saved.

## A minimal MLP in PyTorch: about fifteen lines {h=0a1f2a30}

Fifteen lines are enough: an `nn.Sequential` of linear layers and ReLUs, `BCEWithLogitsLoss` (sigmoid and cross-entropy in one stable function), the Adam optimiser and a loop of `zero_grad`, `backward` and `step`. On 3,576 HMEQ loans the MLP beats the logistic score (AUC 0.945 against 0.920) and boosting beats both; on 1,000 German applicants nothing beats logistic regression significantly. In both cases the cost-based cut-off matters more than the model.
